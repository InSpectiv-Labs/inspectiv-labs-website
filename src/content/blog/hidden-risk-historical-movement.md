When evaluating an asset, most assessments focus on its current condition.

But ground movement doesn’t begin on the day someone notices it.

Deformation may develop gradually over months or years before becoming visible through conventional inspection. By the time movement becomes obvious, the underlying process may already be well established.

This raises an important question:
**What if we could look back in time?**

Satellite-based InSAR makes that possible.

## The value of looking backwards

Modern Earth observation satellites have been collecting radar imagery repeatedly over the same regions for years.

This creates a valuable historical record.

By analysing archived SAR observations, organisations can investigate how the Earth’s surface has behaved over time.

Instead of having only a snapshot of present conditions, stakeholders can potentially establish a **historical deformation timeline**.

For example:
- When did movement begin?
- Where was it concentrated?
- Was it stable or accelerating?
- Did the pattern change after a particular event?
- Are current deformation patterns consistent with historical behaviour?

These questions can be particularly valuable when assessing physical risk.

## Why history matters for asset owners

Consider a large mining operation.

A current inspection may identify an area exhibiting deformation.

But knowing that movement exists is only part of the picture.

Historical analysis can help determine whether the area:
- Has been moving consistently for several months
- Recently began deforming
- Has experienced increasing rates of movement
- Previously exhibited similar behaviour
- Is part of a larger deformation pattern

That additional context can help engineers prioritise investigation and understand changing site conditions.

The same principle applies well beyond mining.

### Historical intelligence across industries

**Infrastructure**
For major infrastructure projects, historical deformation can help establish baseline ground behaviour.
This can be useful when investigating areas around construction, tunnelling or transportation corridors.

**Oil & Gas and Energy**
For long-distance pipelines and energy assets, historical analysis can help screen extensive corridors for persistent ground movement.
This can help identify locations requiring closer inspection.

**Urban Development**
Historical deformation information can provide context around subsidence and ground movement in rapidly developing urban areas.
It can also help distinguish persistent deformation patterns from isolated observations.

## An additional layer for lenders and insurers

Historical ground movement can be particularly relevant when the person assessing an asset isn’t the person operating it.

A lender, insurer or investor may have limited visibility into an asset’s historical physical behaviour.

Satellite-derived historical information can provide an independent data layer that complements:
- Engineering reports
- Site inspections
- Maintenance records
- Existing monitoring systems
- Geological assessments

It does not replace these sources.

Instead, it can provide another perspective.

For example, a lender evaluating a large infrastructure asset may want to understand whether areas surrounding that asset have demonstrated persistent ground movement.
An insurer assessing physical risk may want historical context when evaluating an exposure.
A geotechnical consultant may use historical deformation patterns to support further investigation.

## From historical data to better questions

The greatest value of historical analysis is often not a single number.

It is the **questions that the data enables organisations to ask**.

Instead of:
“There is deformation here.”

the analysis can move towards:
- “When did this deformation begin?”
- “How has it evolved?”
- “Is it persistent?”
- “Is it concentrated around a particular asset?”
- “Does the pattern justify further investigation?”

That is a much more useful starting point for risk assessment.

## Retrospective analysis: learning from what has already happened

Historical InSAR can also be used retrospectively.

When an incident occurs, organisations naturally want to understand what happened before it.

Was there measurable deformation?
When did it begin?
Was the movement localised?
Did the deformation pattern change before the incident?

Retrospective analysis cannot rewrite the past.

But it can help organisations understand whether satellite observations contained signals that could have provided additional situational awareness.

This type of analysis can also inform how future monitoring programmes are designed.

## Looking backwards to monitor forwards

The greatest opportunity isn’t simply using historical data to explain past events.

It is using historical understanding to establish better monitoring strategies going forward.

A historical assessment can help establish:
**Baseline → Change → Trend → Investigation → Ongoing Monitoring**

That creates a more informed foundation for continuous asset monitoring.

## The bigger picture

Physical asset risk is not static.

The ground beneath an asset can change continuously, sometimes imperceptibly.

By combining historical satellite observations with engineering knowledge and current monitoring, organisations can develop a much richer understanding of how their assets and surrounding environments evolve.

Because sometimes the most important information about today’s risk is hidden in yesterday’s data.
